**Claude:**

That's a clear enough request, and a common one — no need to interrogate it before being useful. So here's the practical answer.
The thing most adults get wrong about making friends is treating it as a matter of meeting enough people. It's not. Friendship comes from repeated, low-stakes contact with the same people over time. Psychologists who study this point to three ingredients: proximity, repetition, and unplanned interaction. That's why friendships form so easily in school and rarely in adulthood — adult life strips out the repetition.
So the move is to engineer repetition. A one-off event almost never produces a friend. A thing you show up to every week for a couple of months often does. Concretely: pick something recurring with the same roster of people — a class, a volunteer shift, a running club, a board game night, a religious or hobby group, a regular pickup sport. The specific activity matters less than that it repeats and the faces stay constant.
Second: be the one who follows up. Most people feel the spark of "I liked that person" and then do nothing. The friendship goes to whoever is willing to risk the slightly awkward next step — "want to grab coffee?" or "a few of us are getting food after, come along." It feels forward. It mostly isn't. People are generally glad to be asked.
Third: depth comes from small disclosures, traded back and forth. You don't manufacture closeness by sharing something heavy. You build it by being a little more real than small talk each time, and seeing if it's returned.
A reasonable first step this week is to pick one recurring thing and just go once, with no expectation beyond showing up.
One question that would let me sharpen this: is the harder part for you finding people to meet, or turning people you already encounter — coworkers, acquaintances — into actual friends? Those need fairly different advice, so I'd rather aim at the right one.
